Project overview

Modernizing without starting from scratch.

Sysdyne builds software that supports concrete production, distribution, and delivery. Each product had evolved independently - with its own patterns, components, and ways of presenting information.

I joined the team in October 2022 as a UX/UI Designer. My role was to create a scalable design system ecosystem and use it to progressively modernize the entire product portfolio.

The problem

Inconsistency
was more than
a visual
problem.

For almost 15 years, similar features had been designed and implemented in different ways. Many interface decisions were made directly during development, without a shared source of truth.

  • Different patterns across every product
  • Duplicated, one-off components
  • No standardized handoff process
  • Slower design of new features

The goal

Create consistency without taking away each product’s specialized character.

Product ecosystem

Different users. Different working environments.

From dispatchers monitoring multiple deliveries at once to drivers completing a single task in the field — the shared language had to work across entirely different contexts.

One system was not enough.

Desktop applications support complex process monitoring and data-heavy work. Mobile products are used on the move, where speed, clarity, and fewer steps matter most.

01 / ConcreteGO

Complexity made navigable, not invisible.

Concrete operations are inherently complex. Dispatchers and plant teams need to see customers, products, schedules, trucks, quantities, and exceptions at the same time. My goal was not to reduce the amount of information, but to make it easier to scan, understand, and act on.

  • Clear hierarchy across data-dense screens
  • Consistent status and action patterns
  • Progressive disclosure for complex records

Information density became a deliberate tool: structure first, decoration second.

02 / Delivery-Go

A clear picture instead of a wall of data.

Delivery-Go brings delivery progress, fleet activity, and workforce data into one operational environment. The challenge was to help dispatchers recognize what needed attention without stripping away the details required to act.

  • Orders grouped by delivery stage
  • Consistent status and exception signals
  • Shared patterns across logistics and workforce tools

Show the whole operation. Make the next action obvious.

03 / Delivery-Go for drivers

Not a smaller desktop. A tool for the road.

The tablet experience follows drivers through an entire shift — from understanding the current delivery to vehicle inspections and maintenance. Each screen prioritizes one task, large touch targets, and an unmistakable next step.

Consistency does not mean uniformity. The interface adapts to the device, the environment, and the urgency of the task.

One shift / multiple focused tasks

One screen. One primary task. One obvious next step.

Mobile system in practice

Responsive by behavior, not only by size.

The mobile library defined complete interaction patterns, component states, and responsive transformations. Dense desktop structures were rethought for touch, limited screen space, and fast decision-making in the field.

Preserve the meaning of the information. Adapt how users act on it.

04 / Batch-Go

A shared language for a specialized tool.

Batch-Go brought the design system into the production plant. The interface had to represent a physical process, surface critical deviations, and give operators direct access to controls without losing the shared product language.

  • Live production state at a glance
  • Target-versus-actual material tracking
  • Operational data connected to ticket history

Make the physical process visible. Surface deviations before they become problems.

Collaboration

The system became the team’s shared language.

I worked with a Product Manager in the US and developers in Warsaw. Components were designed and validated on real product screens rather than in an isolated library.

A structured handoff moved decisions about interface behavior away from the implementation stage and into a shared, documented system.

Consistency does not mean uniformity.

An effective design system provides shared principles while leaving room for the device, working context, and complexity of the user’s task.
